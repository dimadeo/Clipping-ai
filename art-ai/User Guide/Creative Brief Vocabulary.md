# Creative brief vocabulary

A practical vocabulary for The Dark Matters. Use these words to describe what you want to see, not simply to make a prompt longer. The meanings below are working definitions for creative briefs; an image model may interpret them differently.

Each entry gives a meaning and a short phrase you can adapt. Choose a few compatible terms from different sections. Strong briefs usually describe the subject, composition, medium, light, palette and mood clearly.

## Build a richer brief in seven parts

1. **Subject:** What is shown?
2. **Composition:** Where is it placed, how close is it, and what stays empty?
3. **Material:** What surface or artistic medium should it resemble?
4. **Light:** Where does the light come from and how soft is it?
5. **Palette:** Which colours and tonal limits define the work?
6. **Mood:** What should the viewer feel?
7. **Consistency and exclusions:** What must remain consistent, and what must be absent?

### Copyable brief template

> Create [subject] in a [composition and viewpoint]. Use the appearance of [medium and texture], illuminated by [lighting]. Limit the palette to [colours and tonal direction]. The mood should feel [two complementary mood words]. Make [element] the focal point. Preserve [important detail or collection rule]. Exclude [unwanted elements]. Compose for [aspect ratio].

## Three complete examples

### Monochrome portrait collection

> Create a close head-and-shoulders portrait of an adult woman, frontal and at eye level, with natural facial proportions and direct eye contact. Make the eyes the focal point, with crisp iris detail and small natural catchlights. Use neutral black, white and silver-grey tones, preserving detail in both skin highlights and dark hair. Combine soft side lighting with a deep charcoal background. Suggest subtle varnished oil-paint texture while retaining natural skin detail. The mood is intimate and contemplative. Keep the face scale, crop and lighting consistent across the series. Exclude yellow tint, sepia, lettering, artificial eye glow and waxy skin. Portrait format, 4:5.

### Square botanical study

> Create a single magnolia branch arranged diagonally across a square composition with generous negative space. Show translucent petals with delicate veins, a matte charcoal background and restrained pearlescent highlights. Use soft diffused side light, with selective focus on the central flower. Limit the palette to chalk white, cool grey and muted sage. The mood is serene and understated. Suggest fine paper tooth and gentle painted detail. Exclude frames, room scenery, text and excessive gloss. Square format, 1:1.

### Large landscape abstraction

> Create an abstract landscape of layered biomorphic plaster forms, flowing horizontally with a low focal point and spacious upper field. Use shallow relief, fine mineral grain and restrained palette-knife marks. Grazing light from the upper left should reveal the surface without harsh glare. Limit the palette to warm stone, muted clay and charcoal accents. The mood is meditative and monumental. Preserve a clear visual hierarchy and slow rhythmic curves. Exclude buildings, people, lettering and metallic shine. Landscape format, 3:2; the separate delivery target is 150 × 100 cm.

## Avoid conflicting instructions

| Conflicting request | More precise alternative |
| --- | --- |
| Everything sharply focused, with shallow depth of field | Eyes sharp; background softly blurred |
| Pure black and white with golden highlights | Neutral black and white, bright silver-grey highlights |
| Minimalist, with many ornaments everywhere | One ornament as an accent, generous negative space |
| Matte surface with strong mirror reflections | Matte paint with a few satin highlights |
| Identical portraits with completely different lighting | Same lighting across the series; vary the expression |
| Make a square without cropping any part of this portrait | Extend the surroundings to a square while preserving the full portrait |

Start with a clear brief, inspect the result, and change one or two features at a time. Specific feedback such as “keep the gaze; reduce the shine; move the light to the left” is more useful than simply asking for “better quality”.
